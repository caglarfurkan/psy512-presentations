Background · Box 1The imagery debate, 1970s–2000s

Does the brain draw a picture, or write a sentence?

The same content, an apple to the left of a cup, in the two formats the debate was about.

1970s–80sBehavioural evidence for depictive images; each result gets an alternative reading
1990sNeuroimaging moves the fight to one question: does imagery activate V1?
2015Pearson & Kosslyn declare it over: imagery can be depictive and still carry meaning
2019So the question changes: how is an image made, and why is yours not like mine?

Why V1? It is laid out like a map of the visual field. Activity there during imagery would mean a picture-like format.

Toggle between the two formats. Depictive means the representation has a functional space: distances in the representation correspond to distances in the thing. Propositional means a language-like description. The debate is the reason so many studies fixated on V1. Pearson treats it as settled, which frees the review to ask about mechanism and individual differences. Note for discussion later: 'the debate is over' is the author's own 2015 claim.

Imagery is vision running in reverse.

Frontal

Active whatever the content is. Starts and organises the image; does not hold the picture.

Medial temporal · hippocampus

Supplies the material from memory. Probably for complex, spatial scenes; evidence is mixed.

Visual cortex

Where the content appears. Imagery and perception overlap most in high-level areas, least in V1.

1 · Neural mechanismsThe early visual cortex

Does imagining light up V1? Twenty-five years of “sometimes”.

20+fMRI / PET studies, 1990s–2000s
  • Many found no V1 response above baseline. Many others did.
  • Pearson's reading: they differed in task, in content (one feature vs. a full scene), and in how vividly their participants imagine.

Decoding changed the question

  • What you imagine can be read out from V1 and V2, even when overall activity is low.
  • A decoder trained on seeing still works on imagining: same features (location, orientation, spatial frequency).
  • Same division of labour as vision: ventral damage disrupts imagining shape; dorsal damage disrupts imagining location and rotation.
  • The network overlaps the default mode network: resting minds are often imagining.
The mixed V1 results look like a failure to replicate, but Pearson reframes them as unmeasured moderators, especially individual differences in vividness. That argument returns at the end: you cannot interpret a group average if half the group does the task without images. Multivariate decoding matters because amplitude can be low while the pattern is informative. The what/where split sets up aphantasia later.

Method · Box 2Try it · binocular rivalry priming

How do you measure a picture only one person can see?

Readyone trial takes about 11 seconds
Cue3 s
Imagine6 s
Display0.75 s
Report
  • Until 2008: questionnaires. Vividness ratings, since Galton (1880). What we did at the start.
  • The trick: show a different pattern to each eye and only one is seen at a time. Imagining one beforehand makes it more likely to be the one you see.
  • The bias tracks vividness, trial by trial and person by person, and survives a demanding task in between.
  • Not just attention: imagery and attention differ in their timing and in how easily sensory input disrupts them.
  • Also objective: fMRI decoding, and pupil size, which follows the brightness of what is imagined.

Pearson, Clifford & Tong (2008); Laeng & Sulutvedt (2014), as reviewed in Pearson (2019), Box 2.

Run one trial with the room so they feel the structure: a letter tells you what to imagine, six seconds of imagining, then a brief flash of both patterns and a forced choice. Be honest that a projector cannot produce rivalry, because both eyes get the same image, and that the durations here are my demo values, not the paper's. The dependent variable is how often the imagined pattern dominates. This is the paper's key methodological move and the measure behind most of its strong claims, including the ones on aphantasia. Worth flagging: it is also the author's own method.
2 · FindingsImagery and perception

Imagery behaves like a weak form of perception.

It primes

Imagining a pattern biases what you see next, like a faint real stimulus would.

It trains

Practising on imagined lines improves real visual sensitivity to them.

It conditions

An emotional association learned to an imagined stimulus transfers to the real one.

It moves the pupil

Imagined brightness changes pupil size with no light change.

Weak or brief input helps what comes next; strong input suppresses it. Imagery lands on the weak side, and longer imagining primes more.

Curve is a schematic of the argument in Pearson (2019), not plotted data. And still: imagining and seeing feel very different. The paper does not explain why.

Four independent lines of evidence that imagery has sensory consequences. The energy idea ties them together: in perception, weak stimuli facilitate and strong ones adapt; imagery sits with the weak ones. Like perception it is also local in retinotopic space and orientation. Close with the gap Pearson himself admits: functional similarity does not explain why the conscious experience is so different.
1 · Neural mechanisms · Fig. 4Try it

Why is your image stronger than mine?

The visual cortex as a blackboard. An image is chalk from above on a surface that is never quiet.

  • Smaller V1 and V2 → stronger imagery, but less precise.
  • Smaller V1 goes with larger frontal cortex: more control over less surface?
  • Lower resting excitability → stronger imagery. Brain stimulation nudges it up or down.
  • Correlational, and “presently only a theory”.

An illustration of the hypothesis, not a simulation of cortex.

Let someone call out settings, or use the four presets, which are the four corners of Fig. 4. Strong signal on a quiet board is the strongest image; weak signal on a noisy board is the weakest. Two different people can end up with equally poor imagery for opposite reasons. Then the evidence: V1 surface area predicts imagery strength negatively; the same pattern appears in schizophrenia, where V1 is about 25 percent smaller and imagery is more vivid. Stress the caveat: Pearson says himself this is correlational and a theory.

Remember the tilt of each bar

How did you hold it for those five seconds?

  • People report two strategies: keep a detailed picture, or code the features in words.
  • Only good imagers are disturbed by irrelevant visual input during the delay. So only they use the visual cortex for this.
  • Aphantasic people still pass. Imagery is one tool among several.
Run it once: four tilted bars for two seconds, five seconds of nothing, then one bar comes back and the room says same or different. Reveal. Then the real question: how did you hold it? Click each option once per hand. The point is that accuracy alone does not tell you which mechanism was used. Pearson uses this to explain the disagreement in the working-memory literature about whether visual cortex stores the content: it does, in some people.
4 · Mental and neurological disordersBridge to Week 10

The same ability, turned against its owner.

Pictures move emotion more than words do. That makes imagery a symptom and a treatment.

As a symptom

  • PTSD · flashbacks are involuntary imagery; the more vivid one's ordinary imagery, the more flashbacks
  • Depression · trouble forming positive images of the future
  • Bipolar disorder · intrusive imagery, “flash-forwards”
  • Addiction · craving arrives as intrusive images
  • Schizophrenia, Parkinson's · stronger sensory imagery on the rivalry measure; in Parkinson's it predicts hallucination severity

As a treatment

  • Imaginal exposure · imagine the feared thing repeatedly until the anxiety falls
  • Imagery rescripting · replay the scene with a different ending
  • Tetris after trauma footage · keep the visual “blackboard” busy while the memory consolidates, and fewer intrusions follow

Note the pattern from slide 10 again: in schizophrenia V1 is about 25% smaller while the whole brain is about 2% smaller.

Keep this short; week 10 covers clinical contexts in depth, including the Tetris paper and intrusive memories in aphantasia. The logic: visual thought produces more emotion than verbal thought, so vivid imagery amplifies whatever is being simulated, good or bad. The Tetris finding connects directly to the blackboard idea: occupy the board and the intrusive image cannot be replayed.
5 · Extreme differencesOur room, from slide 2

From a blind mind's eye to a photograph.

AphantasiaHyperphantasia

Aphantasia

  • Not just a reporting problem: they also score significantly lower on the rivalry measure, so sensory strength itself is lacking.
  • “What” is gone, “where” is intact: spatial imagery scores are normal, even slightly higher.
  • Life goes on: working memory, episodic memory, and often visual dreams.
  • Unknown: the low end of a spectrum, or a category of its own?

Hyperphantasia · eidetic imagery

  • Photo-like, projected into space, described in the present tense, stays put when the eyes move.
  • Mostly in children: estimates run from 0 to 11%.
  • Almost no modern data: never studied with psychophysics, fMRI or TMS.

If people manage without it, imagery is not fundamental to cognition. It is a strategy.

Bring back the room's distribution. Then aphantasia: named by Zeman in 2015. The key result is that people with aphantasia score significantly lower on the binocular rivalry measure, so it is more than poor metacognition. The dissociation between object and spatial imagery mirrors the ventral and dorsal streams from slide 6. Hyperphantasia gets half a page in the paper because there is very little recent research. The closing implication is the 'unnecessary' in the abstract.
Discussion in the paperThe future of imagery research

Pearson's closing argument: imagery is the cleanest window onto feedback.

Seeing always mixes signals going up with signals coming down. They cannot be pulled apart while the eyes are open.

Voluntary imagery is close to feedback alone, in sensory isolation. Study it, and you study the constructive half of perception.

What the paper leaves open

  • Why does imagery almost never feel as strong as seeing?
  • What causes aphantasia and the wide spread between people?
  • Can imagery be trained?
  • Does imagery drive visual cortex, or only modulate it?
  • Can there be imagery without awareness?
This is the paper's own discussion section. The claim is strong: imagery is 'the key to understanding normal visual perception' because it isolates feedback. Then read the open questions; several of them are picked up by this week's other readings, which is the next slide.
