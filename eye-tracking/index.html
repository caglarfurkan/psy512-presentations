The micro-zone of proximal development, and what to doubt

This paper is different from the others this week: it is not a cognitive-psychology experiment with conditions and statistics. It is a frame-by-frame qualitative analysis of four tutoring sessions, used to put empirical flesh on a hundred-year-old theory. I follow the standard order, and you will do the task yourselves before seeing the results.
Background 1Vygotsky's claim

Vygotsky: teaching/learning as collaboration.

Real form · naive

How the learner perceives and acts now

In this task: the triangle turns green somewhere around here.

→
Ideal form · cultural

How the culture perceives and acts

In this task: those are the points equidistant from a point and a line. A parabola.

Idea 1 · the gardener

Teach by arranging the environment. You do not grow a plant by pulling on it.

Idea 2 · collaboration

Ideal forms first exist between people, then become the individual's own.

Zone of proximal development

What the learner can do with someone, but not yet alone.

The problem for a century: nobody could observe how the tutor and the student were each perceiving the situation while it happened.

Real and ideal are meant narrowly here: the starting and the target way of perceiving and acting. Vygotsky's two lines of thought both matter for this study: the task environment is designed so that the student can discover the rule herself (the gardener), and yet a tutor is indispensable (collaboration). The authors' motivation: these claims were never tested at the level of perception, because there was no instrument. Dual eye tracking is the instrument.
Background 2Education changes perception

Expert and novice perception.

Illustration of the cited findings, not recorded data
  • Same picture, different objects. Novices attend to a line's local details; experts to slope and intercepts.
  • Showing learners an expert's gaze helps in medicine and zoology: where to look.
  • It does not help in geometry and problem solving. There the hard part is how to see.
  • The authors suggest: learning how to see may need more than guidance on where to look.

Arcavi (2003); Gegenfurtner et al. (2011); van Gog et al. (2009), as reviewed in Shvarts & Abrahamson (2019), §2.2.

Toggle novice and expert. The dots are an illustration of what the cited eye-tracking studies report. This is the link to the rest of week 11: eye movements show selection, but two people can fixate the same point and see different things. The key move for this paper: gaze-modelling works where the task is to find the right location, and fails where the task is to construe the display differently. That sets up the claim that mathematical perception has to be grown through the learner's own activity.
Background 3Try it · the Mathematics Imagery Trainer

Action-based embodied design.

Drag the two markers. Find one green position, then find three more.

The screen is green only at one hidden ratio. To keep it green while moving, the hands must travel at different speeds.

Action-based design

Step 1: solve a movement problem with no symbols on screen. Step 2: the tutor brings in the mathematics to describe what the body already does.

Attentional anchor

An imagined structure the learner invents to steer the movement. It shows up as a stable, repeating eye-movement pattern.

After Abrahamson & Trninic (2011), Fig. 1 in the paper. This demo uses a 1:2 ratio.

Let someone find green by trial and error. The naive strategy, keeping a fixed distance between the hands, fails as soon as you move up. Students who succeed start looking at something that is not drawn anywhere (in Abrahamson's other studies, for example the interval between the hands; that example is not in this paper). That invented perceptual object is the attentional anchor, and it is the central construct for the results: the authors identify it from repeating saccade patterns.
Background 4Joint action and joint attention

How do two people end up looking at the same thing?

The classic answer

Gaze following

I check your eyes, work out what you are attending to, and look there.

What dual eye tracking found

Watching the hands

Infants and adults also reach joint attention by looking at the object the other is handling. No mind-reading needed.

  • Observers predict. Watching someone act, the eyes arrive ahead of the hand.
  • Learning is better when the learner leads joint attention and the adult follows.
  • Experts and novices drift toward each other when they play Tetris together.

The authors' hypothesis: tutoring relies on this sensorimotor coordination even when it is not visible.

Yu & Smith (2016, 2017); Flanagan & Johansson (2003); Tomasello & Farrar (1986); Jermann et al. (2010), as reviewed in §2.4.

Two routes to joint attention. The second route matters here: the partner's action on an object is a simple, ecologically available cue. Add anticipation: when we watch an action, our gaze leads it. The authors combine these into 'intersubjective coupling of perception-action systems': student and tutor as one distributed system. The last line is the hypothesis the empirical part is meant to support.
Research questions§2.5

What evidence of coupling can be found in two people's gaze, and what does it do for learning?

If the theory is right, we should see……which would stand for
1Student and tutor eye movements that are sometimes alike and sometimes notReal vs. ideal forms of perceiving the same display
2Their gaze becoming synchronous and landing on the same placeJoint attention: how it starts and how it is kept
3The tutor's gaze tied to what the student's hand is doingCoupling of the two into a single system

No hypotheses to reject and no conditions to compare. These are things to look for.

The authors call the model neo-Vygotskian: pedagogical interaction as dynamical coupling between two perception-action systems. The three questions are operationalisations rather than testable predictions. That is worth saying out loud, because it shapes how much the results can show.
Method§3

Method: dual eye tracking.

  • Instrument: two head-mounted Pupil Labs eye trackers, 60 Hz (one sample every ~17 ms), scene camera 30 Hz
  • Novelty: both gaze paths drawn onto one video of the shared screen, with hands and gestures
  • Participants: four psychology undergraduates, 17–21, no memory of the parabola definition
  • Tutors were told: help, but never give the solution
Four dyads from four students: each first student then tutors the next
ResearchertutorsStudent 1then tutorsStudent 2× 2

Task and analysis

  • Stage 1: “Make the triangle green”, then “move it so it stays green”. The only stage analysed in this paper
  • Stage 2: axes appear; work out the formula of the curve
  • Stage 3: a grid; predict green points from the formula
  • Analysis: micro-ethnography. Frame by frame through video, audio and both gaze paths. The unit is the dyad, not the individual.

Calibration: 5 points each, before the task. The student moves the triangle with a finger on a touchpad.

Give the numbers plainly, they matter for the critique. Sixty hertz means 17 milliseconds between samples. The chain design is clever: a student who has just learned the task becomes the tutor, so there are four dyads, two with the researcher as tutor and two with a peer. The authors report the same phases in all four. No quantitative coding is reported. Next slide: you do stage 1.

Method · Fig. 3Try it · your mouse is the eye

How to read a gaze path.

  • The circle is where the eye is now.
  • The line is the last two seconds.
  • Each dot is one sample, 17 ms apart.
  • Dots piled into a knot: a fixation.
  • Dots strung out evenly: smooth pursuit of something moving.
  • A long bare line: a saccade.

Move the mouse over the black area as if it were an eye: rest, follow something slowly, jump. The trail is drawn exactly the way the paper draws gaze: sixty samples a second, two seconds of history. Three signatures to remember for the results: knots are fixations, evenly spaced dots are pursuit, long gaps are saccades. Every result figure in the paper is a still of two such trails, yellow for the student and red for the tutor.
Results · Phase 1 of 4Convergence

Phase 1: the tutor's gaze follows the student's action.

Schematic reconstruction of Figs. 6–8 · lag exaggerated

The student hunts for green spots. The triangle is mostly blue.

  • Tutor's gaze trails the student's by ~17 ms, sometimes 30–60. A reaction to a new stimulus takes 80–150 ms
  • The student's eyes plan: jump ahead, wait ~600 ms, then the hand follows.
  • At a surprise turn the tutor falls 200–300 ms behind, then locks on again.

Authors' reading: joint attention emerges from joint action, with the student leading.

In this phase the two gaze paths are nearly on top of each other. The authors' argument: a lag far shorter than saccadic reaction time means the tutor is anticipating, not reacting. The tutor is not reading the student's eyes; both are coupled to the same moving object, which acts like an extension of the student's body. Switch to student only to show the look-ahead: gaze goes to where the triangle will be moved. Keep in mind for the critique: 17 milliseconds is exactly one sample.
Results · Phase 2 of 4Divergence

Phase 2: student and tutor gaze diverge.

Schematic reconstruction of Fig. 9

The student can now find green, shakily. No rule yet.

  • Student: smooth pursuit of the corner being moved.
  • Tutor: quick back-and-forth saccades along the median or a side, restarting from wherever the student puts the corner.
  • An “inert” attentional anchor: the eye pattern of someone keeping it isosceles, without moving anything.

Authors' reading: the gazes are out of sync, but the tutor's gaze is still tied to the student's action.

This is the paper's most interesting observation. As soon as the triangle is green, the tutor's gaze stops following and starts measuring: saccades from the moving vertex down the median or along a side, as if checking the two equal lengths. The tutor is rehearsing how she would do it herself. Real and ideal forms now coexist in the same activity. The authors speculate about the function: the tutor keeps comparing the student's performance to the ideal and waits for the right moment to step in.
Results · Phase 3 of 4Embodied discovery

Phase 3: the student develops an attentional anchor.

Schematic reconstruction of Figs. 10–11

The tutor only encourages: “faster”, “smoother”. Never says where to look.

  • Movement becomes fluent, quicker and more accurate.
  • Student: pursuit gives way to repeating saccades along the median or a side. An attentional anchor has formed.
  • Not the tutor's pattern. In one dyad the student runs the median while the tutor checks one side, then the other.

Authors' reading: the anchor came from the designed task, not direct instruction. It does not yet mean the student knows the rule.

The student arrives at an anchor through her own practice inside the designed task: Vygotsky's gardener. Important detail: the student's and tutor's anchors need not be the same pattern. What has to be shared in the end is the mathematical meaning, not the scan path. Having an anchor does not mean being aware of the rule, which is why phase 4 is needed.
Results · Phase 4 of 4Conceptual discovery

Phase 4: the tutor's prompt in two dyads.

Dyad 1 · it works

“Could you think about the triangle?”

Not equilateral… isosceles. “Two sides of equal length.”

She names the rule first and only then looks along the sides.

The tutor points at nothing. She reframes what the student is already looking at.

Dyad 2 · too early

“Can you think of a rule that keeps it green?”

“This angle will be the same.”

Even at the bottom, where it is almost a line?

“Let me check it!”

Her anchor worked on the left and right branches only. More practice across the bottom; the next prompt succeeds.

Micro-zone of proximal development: the authors' term for the moment when the student moves fluently and a tutor's prompt can lead to the concept.

Dialogue shortened from the transcripts in §4.4.

Two cases make the argument. In the first, the student is fluent, has an anchor, and a question about 'the triangle' as a whole is enough: the concept was already there in action. In the second the tutor asks before the student has integrated the bottom of the curve into the same movement scheme; she gives a non-normative rule about an angle, and the tutor sends her back to practise. The authors read the failure as the tutor not attending to the student's micro-history. The term they introduce is the micro-zone of proximal development.

Throughout, the tutor's perception stays tied to the student's action. That constant link, not matching gaze, is what the authors call coupling.

Sync bars are my qualitative summary; the paper reports no synchrony measure.

The summary the paper builds toward. Synchrony of gaze is high only at the start. What persists across all four phases is that the tutor's eye movements depend on what the student's hand is doing. The authors conclude that the two form a single distributed system, and that the tutor's silent tracking is what lets her find the micro-zone. Note the honest label on the bars.
Discussion in the paperEducational applications

Educational implications.

For tutors

Watch the action before you speak. The useful prompt works on what the learner is already looking at.

For interface cues

Arrows, highlights and animations move attention to a new place. The paper suggests giving new meaning to the current place instead.

For educational technology

Judge multiple screens and adaptive systems by whether they keep a shared operational point, which joint attention grows from.

Open questions, by the authors' own account: when does this coupling arise, in which other settings, and how often does reframing actually work?

The practical message is a design principle, and it is one I find directly relevant to interface design and to HCI: most attention-guiding techniques redirect. The authors also say why automated tutors struggle with this genre: the right intervention depends on the moment-to-moment history of the learner's movement. They end with the limits of generality, which leads into the critique.
